Section 1of 23

What CLPR is for, and what you can build

Source: design §1 · design §3.4 · design §2.2 (clpr-spec@945082d)

CLPR is a transport primitive: reliable, asynchronous, in-order delivery of arbitrary byte payloads from an application on one ledger to an application on another, with exactly one response per data message. It is defined by what it refuses to introduce. No new token. No intermediary consensus layer. No federated signer set. No pooled liquidity. The destination ledger accepts a message only after verifying, in its own execution environment, a state proof produced by the source ledger’s own consensus. When both ledgers provide asynchronous Byzantine fault tolerant finality, cross-ledger communication inherits those guarantees and “requires only a single honest endpoint node per ledger for correct operation”.

In one paragraph: an application calls sendMessage. The message is appended to an ordered, per-channel outbound queue and folded into a SHA-256 running hash. An off-chain endpoint fetches (or constructs) a proof that the queue state is committed on the source ledger, hands it to a peer endpoint over gRPC, and the peer submits it as a native transaction. The destination CLPR Service passes the opaque proof to the channel’s verifier contract, then independently checks progress, size, sequence and the running hash, dispatches to the application, charges the connector for the gas used, and enqueues a response. The response travels back the same way. The simulation below is the same twenty-eight-step round trip as the general page’s, with the protocol wording under each step and a source line per step.

What people build on it falls into four patterns, each just messages with replies:

Remote smart contract call

A contract on one ledger calls a proxy, which records the call. An endpoint carries it across, the proxy on the other ledger invokes the target contract, and the result comes back the same way. Applications have to be written for answers that arrive later, not in the same call.

Escrow, mint, then burn

Assets are locked on the source ledger, then minted on the destination. If minting succeeds the locked assets may be burned; if it fails they are released. Needs atomic semantics and a common asset standard.

N-ledger atomic swap

Several ledgers make state changes tied together by two-phase commit: each enters a ready-or-roll-back state, and if any one fails, all roll back. Every participating ledger must understand the others' commit messages.

Native Hiero asset management

Hiero tokens, fungible and non-fungible, handled across Hiero networks by the messaging layer directly, with no smart-contract proxies in between.

When several channels exist between the same two ledgers, for example one per Ethereum commitment level, the application picks one at send time by its own needs: a price feed may accept the risk of a reorganisation for speed, a high-value transfer waits for finality. CLPR imposes no default.

Source: design §3.4.1 · design §3.4.2 · design §3.4.3 · design §3.4.4 · design §3.4 (clpr-spec@945082d)

Choosing a channel is part of the application’s design, not the protocol’s: several channels may join the same two ledgers with different verifiers, and the application names one on every send.

Key point

CLPR is a transport primitive: ordered, proven, paid-for bytes from an application on one ledger to an application on another, with exactly one reply per data message. Everything you build is a meaning you give to those bytes.

Section 2of 23

The mental model: the trust chain, safety and liveness

Source: design §1 · design §2.3 · spec §8.2 (clpr-spec@945082d)

Removing the intermediary does not remove trust; it shortens the list. An application on Ledger A sending to Ledger B trusts, in order:

  1. Its own ledger’s consensus: Ledger A orders and finalizes correctly.
  2. The local CLPR Service: the implementation on A executes the protocol faithfully.
  3. The channel’s verifier: the contract that validates B’s proofs. “A buggy or compromised verifier could accept fabricated proofs.”
  4. The remote ledger’s consensus and CLPR Service.
  5. The remote application.

Two infrastructure roles sit outside the chain: the CLPR Service Admin, with purely protective powers (configure, close) and no economic incentive; and the Endpoint Operator, who moves bundles for every channel on a ledger without choosing among them.

Gotcha

Channel creation is permissionless and there is no verifier allow-list. “A Channel existing on a ledger does not mean the peer is legitimate or the verifier is honest.” Anyone can register a channel that claims to be Hedera mainnet with a verifier that accepts anything. Applications must evaluate the verifier contract behind a channel before using it, and connector operators, whose stake is at risk, are expected to do so most carefully.

Endpoints are untrusted for safety, required for liveness. “Even with dishonest endpoints, no fabricated messages can be accepted — but availability depends on at least one honest endpoint per side relaying bundles.” A malicious connector, likewise, “cannot forge or tamper with messages” but can refuse to pay.

Key point

Removing the intermediary does not remove trust. An application trusts its own ledger, its CLPR Service, the channel’s verifier, the remote ledger and its service, and the remote application, in that order.

The four layers

Source: design §3 · organization profile (clpr-spec@945082d, org-profile@263fb39)

Network communication uses gRPC; every protocol type on the wire is protobuf. All cryptographic verification is delegated to verifier contracts registered per channel; the CLPR Service itself is proof-system-agnostic and never interprets proof bytes. The CLPR Service never interprets proof bytes; every cryptographic check belongs to the verifier contract registered for the channel.

Section 4of 23

Send, and two callbacks

Source: design §3.4 · spec §6.4 · spec §6.5 Application Delivery · IClprApplication.sol (clpr-spec@945082d, clpr-smart-contracts@276c8d5)

The application surface is deliberately tiny: one call to send, two callbacks to receive.

sendMessage([auth] caller, channel_id: bytes(32), connector_id: bytes(32),
            target_application: bytes, message_data: bytes) → { message_id: uint64 }
interface IClprApplication {
    /// Called when a cross-ledger Data Message arrives. Return bytes become the reply.
    function onClprMessage(bytes32 channelId, bytes calldata sender, bytes calldata messageData)
        external returns (bytes memory responseData);

    /// Called, best-effort, when the response to a message this contract sent arrives.
    function onClprResponse(bytes32 channelId, uint64 messageId, uint8 status, bytes calldata responseData)
        external;
}

Platform specifications define the gas budget, whether callbacks are synchronous, how success is signalled and what happens to externally owned senders: an EOA cannot receive a callback, so responses to it “SHOULD be recorded in the transaction receipt/record but no callback is made”. In the EVM implementation the callback is synchronous within the bundle transaction, capped at max_gas_per_message, and a revert becomes APPLICATION_ERROR with the revert data as the reply.

A minimal echo application, with the caller check that any real deployment needs:

contract PingPong is IClprApplication {
    address public immutable clprService;
    event MessageReceived(bytes32 indexed channelId, bytes sender, bytes messageData);
    event ResponseReceived(bytes32 indexed channelId, uint64 messageId, uint8 status, bytes responseData);

    constructor(address _clprService) { clprService = _clprService; }

    function serve(bytes32 channelId, bytes calldata connectorId, bytes calldata target, bytes calldata data)
        external returns (uint64 messageId)
    { return IClprService(clprService).sendMessage(channelId, connectorId, target, data); }

    function onClprMessage(bytes32 channelId, bytes calldata sender, bytes calldata messageData)
        external returns (bytes memory)
    {
        require(msg.sender == clprService, "only CLPR service");   // both callbacks are public
        emit MessageReceived(channelId, sender, messageData);
        return messageData;                                          // the returned bytes go in the reply
    }

    function onClprResponse(bytes32 channelId, uint64 messageId, uint8 status, bytes calldata responseData)
        external
    {
        require(msg.sender == clprService, "only CLPR service");
        emit ResponseReceived(channelId, messageId, status, responseData);
    }
}
Gotchas that cost people time
  • Guard both callbacks. They are public functions. Without the msg.sender check, anyone can call your contract directly and bypass the relay entirely.
  • target_application is raw address bytes. On EVM that is exactly 20 bytes. ABI-encoding the address pads it to 32 and the message is dropped.
  • Responses reach contracts, not EOAs. Route sends through a contract if you need onClprResponse.
  • channel_id and connector_id are configuration, not constants. Ongoing obligations: monitor connector health and channel status, and be ready to migrate when a proof format changes.
  • Every payload is untrusted input, in both directions. CLPR proves origin and integrity, “not semantic correctness”.
  • Nothing is confidential. Encrypt at the application layer if you must, knowing that timing, size and parties still leak.
  • Choose the channel by finality need. A price feed might accept a latest-level channel; a token bridge should not.

Reply statuses. There are four: SUCCESS, APPLICATION_ERROR, CONNECTOR_NOT_FOUND and CONNECTOR_UNDERFUNDED. The last two are connector-attributable and slash, and so does a connector charge that fails after the call. The EVM ClprTypes.ReplyStatus adds REDACTED, CHANNEL_CLOSED (a Data Message arriving on a closing channel) and NOT_HANDLED; treat any value beyond the four as implementation-specific.

Key point

sendMessage returns a message_id; that id is what correlates the eventual response with the message you sent.

Getting in line · step 1 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

The application calls sendMessage on the CLPR Service

One function call on the application. It hands over four things: which channel to use, which connector is paying, who the message is for, and the message itself. The application does no cross-chain work of its own; it passes all of that to the CLPR Service and gets back a message id.

In protocol terms. The application calls IClprService.sendMessage(channelId, connectorId, targetApplication, messageData). The service stamps msg.sender as the message's sender and, on success, returns the new message id. The demo application's serve() is a thin wrapper around this call.

Source: design §3.3.2 · spec §4.3

Getting in line · step 2 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

Channel ACTIVE; payload and queue-depth checks

Before accepting anything, the service checks the obvious. Does this channel exist and is it switched on? Is the message small enough for the other side to accept? Is the outbound queue already full? Any "no" and the whole transaction is rejected.

In protocol terms. Enqueue algorithm steps 1, 4 and 5: the Channel must be ACTIVE; len(message_data) must not exceed the peer's advertised max_message_payload_bytes; next_message_id − acked_message_id must be below max_queue_depth. Any failure rejects the send.

Source: spec §4.3 · design §3.1.1

Getting in line · step 3 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

Connector queue quota checked

Each payer gets its own slice of the outbound queue, so one busy application cannot fill the queue and starve everyone else who shares the channel.

In protocol terms. The core specification names queue monopolization as an open issue and lists per-Connector quotas as one mitigation; the EVM reference implementation enforces a per-connector queue quota before asking the connector to authorize. Treat this step as implementation behaviour, not a protocol invariant.

Source: design §3.3.5 · spec §8.11

Getting in line · step 4 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

The connector authorizes the message

The connector is the account that pays. The service asks it, in effect: are you willing to cover this one? If it says no, nothing is sent.

In protocol terms. Enqueue step 3: the service calls the Connector's authorization contract. By returning true the source Connector commits that its counterpart on the destination has funds to pay for execution, and that it can pay for the response. That commitment is what slashing later enforces.

Source: spec §3.3 · design §3.3.1

Getting in line · step 5 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

A ConfigUpdate is queued first if this ledger's settings changed

If anything about this ledger's published limits changed since the last time this channel told the other side, a small housekeeping message is slipped into the queue ahead of yours. On a normal day nothing has changed and this step does nothing.

In protocol terms. Enqueue step 1a, lazy config propagation: if Channel.last_config_timestamp is behind the current configuration's consensus timestamp, a CONTROL ConfigUpdate message is enqueued before the Data Message. Total ordering means the peer applies the new limits at a consensus-determined point in the stream.

Source: spec §1.3 · spec §4.3

Getting in line · step 6 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

Message stored, running hash chained, next id advanced

The message is written into the outbound queue and given the next number in line. It is also folded into a running hash of everything ever sent on this channel, so the other side can later prove that nothing was inserted, removed or reordered.

In protocol terms. Enqueue steps 6–9: build the ClprMessage (connector_id, target_application, stamped sender, message_data); compute running_hash = SHA-256(sent_running_hash ‖ serialized_payload); store it under (channel_id, next_message_id); set sent_running_hash and increment next_message_id.

Source: spec §4.1 · design §3.2.2

Proving it happened · step 7 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

The Hiero network signs the block root

The sending ledger's validators jointly sign the block the message landed in. It is a single signature produced by many nodes together, and they make it as part of producing blocks anyway, not because we asked.

In protocol terms. Consensus signs the block root with an aggregate BLS12-381 threshold signature (hinTS). The endpoint does not construct this: it fetches the finished StateProof, whose BlockStateProof carries ledger_id, tss_signature and the SHA-384 block root.

Source: verifiers/hiero/state_proof.md

Proving it happened · step 8 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

A sync stream opens and BundleRequests are exchanged

The two endpoints keep a connection open between them. The receiving side says which message number it has reached, so the sending side knows exactly what to send.

In protocol terms. A sync cycle is one bidirectional gRPC stream of ClprSyncPayload messages under mutual TLS. Each side's BundleRequest carries its own received_message_id, status, trust_anchor_id and endpoint manifest version. The request is unproven and never changes state; it only shapes a minimal bundle. Delivery is pulled, not pushed.

Source: spec §1.5 · design §3.1.4

Proving it happened · step 9 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

The Hiero endpoint fetches the StateProof

The endpoint collects the receipt proving the message really is in the ledger's signed state. It only fetches this proof. It does not create it, and it could not forge it if it wanted to.

In protocol terms. The endpoint retrieves a StateProof covering the Channel leaf (tag 482) and the anchor ClprMessageValue leaf (tag 498), each with a SHA-384 Merkle path to the signed block root. It parses and validates the structure but does not reassemble it.

Source: verifiers/hiero/state_proof.md

Proving it happened · step 10 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

The BundleResponse travels down the stream

The message, together with its proof, travels down the open line to the other ledger's endpoint.

In protocol terms. A BundleResponse carries one opaque bundle_payload: the ordered batch of messages plus the state proof over the last of them, the anchor. Proving the anchor plus recomputing the running hash chain proves every message in the batch.

Source: spec §1.5 · design §3.2.3

Proving it happened · step 11 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

The EVM endpoint dry-runs the submission

Before spending money submitting anything, the receiving endpoint runs the whole verification locally as a dry run. If it would fail, nobody pays for it.

In protocol terms. Endpoints MUST verify bundle proofs locally before submitting a transaction, otherwise they pay for payloads that fail post-consensus. The EVM relay simulates submitBundle with eth_call and skips the submission if it reverts.

Source: design §3.1.4 · verifiers/qbft/state_proof.md

Crossing · step 12 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

The bundle lands on the EVM CLPR Service

The endpoint sends an ordinary transaction on the destination ledger carrying the message and its proof. Everything from here until the reply is queued happens inside this single transaction.

In protocol terms. Any account may call submitBundle(channelId, proofBytes); submission is permissionless and secured entirely by the channel's verifier. msg.sender is recorded as the submitter and is the sole recipient of connector charges for this bundle.

Source: spec §6.4 · IClprService.sol

Crossing · step 13 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

The verifier checks the threshold signature and the Merkle paths

The destination ledger checks the source ledger's signature itself, against the identity it already trusts, and walks the proof back to the signed block. This is the step that means nobody is trusting a bridge operator.

In protocol terms. Bundle verification step 1: the service calls the Channel's verifier with (bundle_payload, Channel.trust_anchor, channel_context). For a Hiero peer the verifier recomputes the SHA-384 Merkle root from each leaf proof and checks the hinTS aggregate signature against the stored ledger_id, the stored trust anchor is the authority, not the ledger_id inside the proof. It returns sync data, the ordered messages, and the anchor id and running hash.

Source: spec §4.2 · verifiers/hiero/verifier_contract.md

Crossing · step 14 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

Progress, size, replay and running-hash checks

Then the bookkeeping. Is this the next message in sequence? Have we seen it before? Does recomputing the running hash give the same answer as the proven one? Any mismatch and it stops here.

In protocol terms. Steps 1a–4: reject if no progress criterion holds; reject if the bundle exceeds max_messages_per_bundle or any payload exceeds max_message_payload_bytes; the first id must equal received_message_id + 1 with the rest contiguous (replay defence); recomputing SHA-256(prev ‖ payload) from received_running_hash must reproduce anchor_running_hash. These checks are the service's own and hold even if the verifier is buggy.

Source: spec §4.2 · spec §2.1.2

Crossing · step 15 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

Connector resolved and the gas budget computed

The destination looks up the connector that agreed to pay and works out how much execution it can afford.

In protocol terms. The message's connector_id is resolved via (channel_id, connector_id) to the local Connector. A missing Connector yields a CONNECTOR_NOT_FOUND response; an unfunded one is slashed and yields CONNECTOR_UNDERFUNDED. Both are answers, not bundle failures.

Source: design §3.3.3 · spec §4.6

Crossing · step 16 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

The application receives the message and echoes it

The application on the other ledger finally receives the message. It announces it and hands the same payload straight back as its answer. This is the moment the message has actually crossed; everything before it was transport.

In protocol terms. The service dispatches IClprApplication.onClprMessage(channelId, sender, messageData) under a gas cap. The returned bytes become the reply and the status is SUCCESS; a revert becomes APPLICATION_ERROR with the revert data as the reply. An application failing does not fail the bundle.

Source: IClprApplication.sol · design §3.3.3

Crossing · step 17 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

The connector is charged and the submitter reimbursed

The connector pays for the execution that just happened, plus a little extra, and the whole amount goes to whoever submitted the transaction. That margin is why anyone bothers running an endpoint.

In protocol terms. The Connector is charged actual_gas_used × (1 + margin_percent/100). The entire charge is paid to the submitting account; there is no protocol treasury and no payment to anyone on the sending side.

Source: spec §4.6

Crossing · step 18 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

The reply is enqueued as a REPLY message

The answer is placed in the destination ledger's own outbound queue, numbered and folded into the running hash the same way the original was. Every message that gets accepted produces exactly one reply.

In protocol terms. A ClprMessageReply carrying the original message_id, a status and the reply bytes is appended to the destination Channel's outbound queue and chained into its sent_running_hash. Every Data Message produces exactly one Response Message, in order.

Source: spec §1.4 · design §3.2.8

Crossing · step 19 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

The destination announces how far it has got

The destination records how far it has now received and acknowledged. The other side reads those numbers next time they talk and uses them to know what it can safely forget.

In protocol terms. The service's own ChannelSyncData (received_message_id, status, next_message_id) is updated; the EVM implementation also emits a BundleProcessed event once per accepted bundle. The peer learns the new received_message_id on the next sync and treats it as its acked_message_id.

Source: design §3.2.1 · docs/bundle-flow.md

Coming home · step 20 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

The EVM endpoint builds a QBFT state proof

Coming back, the proof works differently. This ledger's validators sign block headers directly, so the endpoint takes a header carrying those signatures plus a proof of the exact storage holding the reply.

In protocol terms. The relay assembles an RLP 5-tuple: the current block header (with QBFT committed seals in extraData), any epoch-boundary headers needed for validator-set rotation, the EIP-1186 account proof for the CLPR Service, storage proofs for five Channel slots and the anchor message's running hash, and the serialized bundle content.

Source: verifiers/qbft/state_proof.md

Coming home · step 21 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

Pre-verification, then delivery to the Hiero endpoint

The same discipline in reverse. Dry-run it first, then send it back down the line.

In protocol terms. The bundle crosses the same stream in the other direction. The receiving endpoint verifies locally before paying to submit.

Source: design §3.1.4

Coming home · step 22 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

Bundle submission on the Hiero ledger

The Hiero-side endpoint hands the reply to its ledger using that ledger's own transaction format rather than an EVM one.

In protocol terms. On Hiero the bundle is submitted as a HAPI transaction and processed post-consensus by the native CLPR Service. The specification does not name a transaction type for this; neither does this page.

Source: design §3.2.3

Coming home · step 23 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

QBFT committed seals checked against the trust anchor

The Hiero ledger checks the other ledger's validator signatures on that block header against the validator list it has on record for this channel, and copes with validators having joined or left since.

In protocol terms. The QBFT verifier recovers the ECDSA signers from the header's committed seals and requires a threshold to match the validator set hash in the trust anchor. Epoch headers in the bundle advance the validator set; each must be sealed by the previous set, so history cannot be skipped.

Source: verifiers/qbft/trust_anchor.md

Coming home · step 24 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

Account and storage proofs, then replay and running-hash checks

Then it checks the proof tying the reply to that exact signed block, and repeats the same sequence and running hash checks as the outbound leg. The proof technology differs; the rules do not.

In protocol terms. The account proof ties the CLPR Service to the header's stateRoot; the storage proofs tie the queue state and anchor hash to that account. Then the service runs the same steps 1a–4 as on the outbound leg. Verification is symmetric; only the proof system changes.

Source: verifiers/qbft/state_proof.md · spec §4.2

Coming home · step 25 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

Response ordering verified against the retained message

Replies have to come back in the same order the messages went out. If one arrives out of turn, the channel pauses rather than delivering it.

In protocol terms. Response ordering verification: the incoming reply's message_id must match the oldest unresponded Data Message still retained in the outbound queue. A mismatch sets the Channel to PAUSED and rejects the bundle; the channel auto-resumes when a correctly ordered bundle arrives.

Source: spec §4.5

Closing the loop · step 26 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

The original application is called back

The original application is called back with the message id, a status, and whatever the other side sent back.

In protocol terms. IClprApplication.onClprResponse(channelId, messageId, status, responseData) is invoked best-effort; the status is a ClprMessageReplyStatus (SUCCESS, APPLICATION_ERROR, CONNECTOR_NOT_FOUND, CONNECTOR_UNDERFUNDED in the spec; the EVM implementation adds a few more).

Source: IClprApplication.sol

Closing the loop · step 27 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

Reply delivered; the original message deleted

Only now is the original message deleted from the outbound queue. It was deliberately kept until its reply arrived, so the ordering check just before had something to compare against.

The message queue and the running hash

Source: spec §1.4 · spec §4.1 · spec §4.3 · design §3.2.2 (clpr-spec@945082d)

One ordered queue per channel per direction carries three message classes in a single intermixed stream, sharing one running hash chain, one proof mechanism and one transport:

message ClprMessagePayload {
  oneof payload {
    ClprMessage        message       = 1;  // DATA: application content
    ClprMessageReply   message_reply = 2;  // RESPONSE: outcome of a Data Message
    ClprControlMessage control       = 3;  // CONTROL: protocol housekeeping (ConfigUpdate)
  }
}
message ClprMessage {
  bytes connector_id       = 1;  // keccak256(channel_id ‖ public_key ‖ salt); same on both ledgers
  bytes target_application = 2;  // destination application address
  bytes sender             = 3;  // stamped by the service from the caller; not caller-supplied
  bytes message_data       = 4;  // opaque
}
message ClprMessageReply {
  uint64                 message_id         = 1;  // the Data Message this answers
  ClprMessageReplyStatus status             = 2;  // SUCCESS | APPLICATION_ERROR | CONNECTOR_NOT_FOUND | CONNECTOR_UNDERFUNDED
  bytes                  message_reply_data = 3;
}
message ClprMessageValue {
  ClprMessagePayload payload                       = 1;
  bytes              running_hash_after_processing = 2;
}

Queue entries are stored separately from the channel, keyed by (channel_id, message_id), because they are read by id range during bundle construction and deleted after acknowledgement. On Hiero they live in a Merkle state map (directly provable); on EVM ledgers in contract storage, where gas cost makes aggressive deletion important.

The running hash

running_hash = SHA-256( previous_running_hash ‖ serialized_payload )     H0 = 0x00 × 32

serialized_payload is the canonical protobuf encoding of the ClprMessagePayload. SHA-256 was chosen for universal availability (EVM precompile, Hiero native, Solana syscall) and retains 128-bit preimage resistance under Grover’s algorithm; the fields are opaque bytes, so an upgrade needs only a protocol version bump.

Enqueue (sendMessage)

  1. Look up the channel; reject unless ACTIVE. 1a. If last_config_timestamp is behind the current configuration’s consensus timestamp, enqueue a ConfigUpdate control message first and update the timestamp.
  2. Look up the connector by (channel_id, connector_id); reject if absent.
  3. Call the connector’s authorization contract; reject if not authorized.
  4. Check len(message_data) ≤ peer_config.throttles.max_message_payload_bytes. A stricter local limit is allowed; exceeding the peer’s is not.
  5. Check next_message_id − acked_message_id < max_queue_depth.
  6. Build the ClprMessage, stamping sender from the transaction caller.
  7. Compute the running hash. 8. Store under (channel_id, next_message_id). 9. Set sent_running_hash; increment next_message_id.

The sender pays only the native transaction fee; there is no protocol fee on the sending side. If anything fails, “the whole thing reverts and the user pays only for the failed attempt”.

Lazy config propagation

When the admin changes the local configuration, nothing is enqueued anywhere; the change is O(1). Each channel picks up a ConfigUpdate the next time it enqueues a message or processes a bundle, at a consensus-determined point in its stream. Messages before the update were valid under the old limits and MUST be accepted; messages after it comply with the new ones. Dead channels never pay for updates. A receiver that meets a control message variant it does not recognize MUST reject the entire bundle: skipping it would let the two sides’ state diverge.

In the EVM reference code

The EVM implementation folds the hash as sha256(prev ‖ sha256(payload)) and checks outbound payload size against its own throttles rather than the peer’s; its docs/bundle-flow.md lists both as known divergences (items C-2, C-3). Its sendMessage stamps msg.sender exactly as required; destination applications may rely on that field but “MUST NOT trust any in-band ‘from’ claim embedded inside messageData itself”.

Key point

Three message classes, one id sequence, one hash chain. A receiver that cannot interpret a control message rejects the whole bundle, because skipping it would let the two sides diverge.

Bundles and progress

Source: design §3.2.3 · spec §2.1.2 · design §3.2.6 · ADR 2026-07-17 (clpr-spec@945082d)

Messages never travel individually. A bundle is an ordered batch of payloads plus one state proof over the last of them, the anchor. The proof covers the running hash at the anchor, so the receiver can verify that the whole batch exists in the sender’s state, in order, with nothing omitted, by recomputing the chain. The endpoint reads unacknowledged messages starting after the peer’s current_received_message_id; if received + 1 ≥ next_message_id the bundle carries no messages. A bundle may be a proper prefix of the backlog, so a queue larger than max_messages_per_bundle or max_sync_bytes can still drain in slices.

    Response ordering and correlation

    Source: design §3.2.8 · spec §4.5 · spec §4.4 (clpr-spec@945082d)

    CLPR distinguishes two confirmations. An acknowledgement is transport-level: the peer’s received_message_id, reported on every sync, becomes the sender’s acked_message_id. A response is application-level: the ClprMessageReply generated when the destination processed a Data Message. Because the destination processes each inbound message sequentially, responses are generated in exactly the order the Data Messages arrived, “regardless of whether each individual response indicates success or failure”.

    That ordering guarantee is what lets the source verify correlation without a separate counter. It walks its retained Data Messages in order and matches each incoming response to the next expected one. Hence the lifecycle rules: Response and Control messages in the outbound queue are deleted as soon as they are acknowledged; Data Messages are retained past acknowledgement and deleted only when their response has arrived and matched.

PAUSED is reserved exclusively for response-ordering violations, because they indicate corruption in the peer's own outbound queue state, not a bad endpoint. Nobody is slashed either way; in the design document's words, you cannot slash a peer ledger.

    Source: design §3.2.8 (the worked example is taken from there) · §3.2.7 · spec §4.5 · §4.4 · §4.2 step 5 · §2.1.1.

    A mismatch is a protocol violation by the peer ledger. The service sets the channel to PAUSED, rejects the bundle outright (nothing dispatched, no acks, no hash advance), and blocks new sendMessage calls; syncs continue, and the channel auto-resumes when a correctly ordered bundle arrives. It does not slash: “you cannot slash a peer ledger, only individual endpoints”, and the out-of-order responses are already committed in the peer’s queue. PAUSED is reserved exclusively for this case; bundles that fail hashing, replay or size checks are simply rejected with no state change.

    Key point

    Acknowledged means delivered; answered means processed. A Data Message leaves the queue only when it has been answered, in order.

    Ledger identity and configuration

    Source: design §3.1.1 · spec §1.1 · spec §7 (clpr-spec@945082d)

    Each ledger publishes one ClprLedgerConfiguration: its identity and the limits it will enforce on peers.

    message ClprLedgerConfiguration {
  uint32        protocol_version        = 1;  // both sides MUST agree; unknown → reject
  string        chain_id                = 2;  // CAIP-2, e.g. "hedera:mainnet", "eip155:1"
  bytes         service_address         = 3;  // this ledger's CLPR Service (contract addr / well-known constant)
  Timestamp     timestamp               = 4;  // consensus time of the last change; monotonic
  ClprThrottles throttles               = 5;
  bytes         initial_trust_anchor    = 6;  // e.g. Hiero ledger_id; empty if derived from a separate proof
  bytes         initial_trust_anchor_id = 7;  // non-empty iff initial_trust_anchor is non-empty
}

    Identity is a compound key. chain_id is a CAIP-2 string, but it does not uniquely identify a CLPR Service: two competing service contracts on Ethereum can both claim eip155:1. A peer is therefore identified by (ChainID, ClprServiceAddress). Private networks may self-assign an unregistered namespace such as hashsphere:acme-prod.

    Protocol version is bumped on any backward-incompatible change: new control message variants, running hash algorithm, signature schemes, wire format. Cross-version messaging is not supported; a peer that receives a ConfigUpdate with an unrecognized version rejects it, halting the channel until it upgrades.

    Throttles are ledger-wide values advertised to all peers and enforced per channel:

    FieldMeaningEnforced by
    max_messages_per_bundleHard cap on messages in one bundle, sized to this ledger’s per-transaction execution budgetSending endpoints must respect it; destination rejects the whole bundle at verification step 2
    max_message_payload_bytesLargest single payload this ledger will acceptSource at enqueue (against the peer’s advertised value); destination at bundle processing (authoritative)

    Gotcha

    The deadlock is real: max_sync_bytes “MUST be greater than max_message_payload_bytes plus the protocol overhead” of a single message. “If max_sync_bytes is set too low, the Channel can deadlock — the next message to send may be larger than the bundle payload can carry, halting progress indefinitely.” The suggested rule of thumb is max_sync_bytes ≥ max_message_payload_bytes × max_messages_per_bundle + proof_overhead.

    Strictness. Every limit is published, so exceeding one is “a measurable, attributable violation”. Implementations MUST reject unrecognized fields, unknown message types and malformed metadata outright rather than skipping them. Defaults for the throttle values are still to be fixed; clprEnabled defaults to false and is checked dynamically on every call, giving each deployment a runtime kill switch.

    Key point

    A peer is (ChainID, ClprServiceAddress): the chain id names a ledger, the address names which service on it.

    Channels

    Source: spec §2.1 · spec §5.1 · design §3.1.3 · contracts docs/lifecycle.md (clpr-spec@945082d, clpr-smart-contracts@276c8d5)

    A channel is the on-ledger record of one peer relationship. Its state is presented as a single structure, while allowing implementations to split it for efficient state proofs:

    Channel {
  channel_id             : bytes(32)   // primary key; same value on both ledgers
  chain_id               : string      // CAIP-2 id of the peer
  peer_config_timestamp  : Timestamp   // last known peer configuration
  verifier_contract      : bytes       // immutable after registration
  verifier_fingerprint   : bytes       // code hash at registration (informational)
  trust_anchor           : bytes       // peer signing authority; only verifyBundle may change it
  trust_anchor_id        : bytes       // opaque id for trust_anchor
  channel_context        : ChannelContext { channel_id, service_address }   // immutable
  endpoint_manifest      : ClprEndpointManifest   // cached peer endpoints (+ version)
  last_config_timestamp  : Timestamp   // for lazy ConfigUpdate propagation
  acked_message_id       : uint64      // highest own message confirmed received by peer
  sent_running_hash      : bytes(32)
  received_running_hash  : bytes(32)
}

    Three fields are deliberately not on the Channel: status, received_message_id and next_message_id. Each side keeps them in its own ChannelSyncData, the only part of the channel that is state-proven and exchanged on every bundle. Wherever a rule says Channel.status, read it as this side’s own ChannelSyncData.status. An ADR moved these fields out so that proofs carry the minimum, not the whole record.

    Initial values. acked_message_id = 0; both running hashes are 32 zero bytes; received_message_id = 0; next_message_id = 1. The first Data Message ever enqueued has ID 1 and the first Response Message received must reference it.

    Registration: commit, then reveal

    Because the same channel_id must be claimed on both ledgers, an attacker who watches a registration on A could race to claim the id on B. The commit-reveal scheme hides both the id and the key until both claims exist. The ownership commitment is keccak256(channel_id ‖ public_key); keccak-256 is fixed cross-platform, while the signature scheme and key encoding are platform-specific (ECDSA secp256k1 or Ed25519 today; “extensible for post-quantum”). Different keypairs may be used on each ledger; the channel id, not the key, ties the two sides together.

    Step 4 · meanwhile

    Someone is watching Ledger A

    An attacker reads Ledger A and sees the registerChannel transaction. All it carries is the 32-byte hash. To claim the same id on Ledger B they would need the channel id; to ever complete a claim they would need the private key. The hash yields neither. Copying the hash onto Ledger B is useless too: completing it requires the preimage and a signature from the key.

    In protocol terms. Anti-squatting rationale: the commitment hides both channel_id and public_key.     Step 5 · Ledger A · reveal

    completeChannel on Ledger A → ACTIVE

    Now the registrant reveals on Ledger A: the channel id, the public key, a signature proving they hold the matching private key, the address of a verifier contract they deployed on A, and two proofs fetched from Ledger B (its CLPR configuration and its endpoint list).     In protocol terms. completeChannel(caller, channel_id, public_key, signature, verifier_contract, config_proof_bytes, endpoint_manifest_proof_bytes), where the signature is over keccak256(channel_id).     Step 6 · Ledger B · reveal

    completeChannel on Ledger B → ACTIVE

    The same reveal on Ledger B, with a verifier deployed on B and proofs fetched from A. Each verifier understands the other ledger's proofs, which is why the two are different contracts.

    In protocol terms. The same inputs work on both ledgers. Note the order the design document's sequence diagram fixes: commitments on both ledgers first, then reveals. The verifier is a locally deployed contract; the Service only requires that it exists on this ledger.

    Source: design §3.1.3 · spec §5.1.3

    Step 7 · done

    Both ACTIVE; the verifier is now fixed for life

    Both sides are ACTIVE and syncing may begin. The verifier each side named cannot be changed later; if the other ledger changes how its proofs look, you register a new channel with a new verifier. The commitment was deleted at reveal, and the registration keypair has no further role in the protocol.

    In protocol terms. Verifier immutability: verifier_contract is immutable after completeChannel; a proof-format upgrade means a new Channel. Initial values on each side: acked_message_id = 0, both running hashes 32 zero bytes, own ChannelSyncData with received_message_id = 0 and next_message_id = 1.     Endpoints and manifests
     
    Source: design §3.1.2 · spec §1.2 · spec §2.4 · spec §6.5 · ADR 2026-07-03 (clpr-spec@945082d)
     
    An endpoint is an off-chain node that runs the sync protocol, obtains proofs, pre-verifies and submits bundles. Two manifests describe them.
    The local manifest (ClprEndpointManifest) is service-level state: an ordered list of ClprEndpoint entries, a version counter starting at 1 and incremented on every change to the set, and the service_address it belongs to. An entry holds an optional ServiceEndpoint (numeric IP and port; omitted by private networks that only dial out) and a tls_certificate: a DER-encoded, self-signed ECDSA P-384 CA certificate, minimized (single-RDN DN, minimal serial, only basicConstraints and keyUsage) and at most 512 bytes, rejected at admission otherwise. That CA is never presented on the wire; each endpoint mints an in-memory Ed25519 leaf signed by it at process start, presents the leaf at every mTLS handshake, and regenerates it on restart or after leaf_certificate_validity_seconds (default 86 400).
    How endpoints get into the local manifest depends on the platform:
      

    • Hiero: every consensus node is automatically an endpoint. The manifest mirrors the roster; a node joining, leaving or rotating its identity bumps the version, stake-weight-only changes do not. No manual administration is required or permitted.
      • 

    • Permissionless ledgers (EVM): two steps. Anyone calls registerEndpoint(endpoint_data, bond) and enters pending; the admin calls addEndpoint to admit it to the live manifest (or updateEndpointManifest for a batch, one version bump for the whole batch). removeEndpoint by the endpoint or the admin returns the bond in full. Endpoint bonds are never slashed; misbehavior means eviction with a refund. Slashing applies only to connectors.
      • 

    The peer manifest is stored per channel as Channel.endpoint_manifest: the authoritative directory of remote endpoints to dial. It is populated at completeChannel from the manifest proof passed to verifyConfig, truncated to max_peer_endpoints, and refreshed whenever verifyBundle returns a new_endpoint_manifest with a higher version, replacing the whole manifest atomically (verification step 1b).
     Manifest updates bypass the queue 
    Manifest updates deliberately do not travel through the message queue. Before ADR 2026-07-03, the endpoint list rode inside ConfigUpdate control messages, which deadlocked on complete endpoint turnover: the update that named the new endpoints could only be delivered by contacting the old ones. Now anyone can call getEndpointManifest() on the remote service, wrap it in a state proof, and submit it as an ordinary transaction on the local ledger; a bundle carrying only a manifest update satisfies progress criterion 3. This is recovery scenario R1.
     
    Bundle submission is permissionless and orthogonal to manifest membership: anyone may submit a bundle that makes progress, whether or not they registered, and the bond plays no role in reimbursement. Sybil resistance on permissionless ledgers is “provided primarily by admin curation”; the bond is “commitment friction”, not a threshold calibrated against attack cost. Endpoints SHOULD randomize peer selection and SHOULD prefer peers that reciprocate with messages of their own, which “cannot be faked — providing a valid bundle with messages requires having actual messages to send”.
    Seen from the endpoint’s side, the whole job is a loop: watch the home queue and the peer manifest, open a connection per channel, ask for what is missing, dry-run what arrives, submit only what would pass.
     Key point 
    The bond buys a seat on a curated list and nothing else: it is never slashed, and anyone may submit a bundle without one.

    The sync protocol
     
    Source: spec §1.5 · design §3.1.4 · ADR 2026-07-14 (clpr-spec@945082d)
     
    Every endpoint exposes one gRPC service with one method:
    service ClprEndpointService {
  rpc sync(stream ClprSyncPayload) returns (stream ClprSyncPayload);
}

message ClprSyncPayload {
  bytes          channel_id      = 1;  // exactly 32 bytes
  BundleRequest  bundle_request  = 2;  // absent when nothing more to request
  BundleResponse bundle_response = 3;  // absent when carrying no bundle
}
message BundleRequest {                // UNPROVEN: shapes the peer's bundle, never changes state
  uint64            current_received_message_id       = 1;
  ClprChannelStatus current_status                    = 2;
  bytes             current_trust_anchor_id           = 3;
  uint64            current_endpoint_manifest_version = 4;
}
message BundleResponse { bytes bundle_payload = 1; }   // opaque; for the verifier
    A sync cycle is one bidirectional stream scoped to a single channel. Each side sends a BundleRequest describing its own state; the responder builds a minimal bundle starting at current_received_message_id + 1; a payload with both fields absent closes the stream. The connection and its TLS session may persist across cycles.

    A sends the first message: a BundleRequest with four values saying where A stands on this channel: how far it has got with B's messages, its channel status, the trust anchor it currently holds for B, and the version of B's endpoint list it has cached.     A verifies, submits to Ledger A, then sends B's bundle
     
    A does not trust the bytes. It runs the verifier locally first, so it does not pay for a bundle whose proof would fail, then submits the bundle as an ordinary transaction to Ledger A. There the CLPR Service checks that the bundle makes progress, that the message ids continue exactly where A's record ends, and that the running hash matches the proven anchor; it records B's acknowledgement and dispatches the messages. Then A builds B's bundle from Ledger A, shaped by B's request, and sends it. A has nothing more to ask, so bundle_request is absent.

    B verifies, submits to Ledger B, and closes the stream
     
    B does the same on its side. With nothing left to ask or send, it ends the cycle with an empty payload: both fields absent. The stream closes; the connection beneath it may stay open for the next cycle, with no new handshake. How often to sync is left to each endpoint. Endpoints also keep score: a peer that only ever asks and never brings messages of its own gets deprioritised.
     
    In protocol terms. ClprSyncPayload(no bundle_request, no bundle_response) closes the stream. Reciprocity-based peer selection: endpoints SHOULD prefer peers that reciprocate by providing messages in return; one that only requests is deprioritised, and this cannot be faked, since a valid bundle with messages requires real messages to send. Sync cadence is deliberately unspecified.

    Three properties to internalize.     Verifiers and trust anchors
     
    Source: spec §3.1 · spec §3.2 · design §3.1.5 · verifiers/ (clpr-spec@945082d)
     
    A verifier implements two read-only methods. It holds no per-channel state: mutable trust material comes in and goes out through the trust_anchor parameters, and constant identity through ChannelContext.
    struct ChannelContext { bytes channel_id; bytes service_address }   // immutable per channel

interface IClprVerifier {
  function verifyConfig(bytes config_proof_bytes, bytes channel_id, bytes endpoint_manifest_proof_bytes)
    returns (ChannelContext ctx, string chain_id, uint96 peer_config_nanos, ClprThrottles throttles,
             bytes initial_trust_anchor, bytes initial_trust_anchor_id, ClprEndpointManifest endpoint_manifest)

  function verifyBundle(bytes bundle_payload, bytes trust_anchor, ChannelContext ctx)
    returns (ChannelSyncData sync_data, ClprMessagePayload[] messages,
             uint64 anchor_message_id, bytes anchor_running_hash,
             bytes new_trust_anchor, bytes new_trust_anchor_id, ClprEndpointManifest new_endpoint_manifest)
}
    verifyConfig runs once, at completeChannel; it MUST revert if any proof fails, if the manifest’s service_address does not match, or if the manifest version is 0, and MUST NOT revert merely because the endpoint list is empty. verifyBundle runs on every submission and “is responsible for validating that the bundle data belongs to the (trust_anchor, ctx.service_address, ctx.channel_id) triple”; hash chain, ordering and replay are the service’s job. EVM verifiers SHOULD fail fast on malformed input before expensive cryptography. ChannelContext exists because channel identity must not live in a field designed to rotate: ADR 2026-07-02 also documents a narrow cross-channel substitution window this closed.
    Trust anchors and rotation
    The trust anchor is opaque to the service: “it stores, passes, and replaces the anchor, but never inspects its contents.” It is initialized from verifyConfig and changes through exactly one path, a non-empty new_trust_anchor returned by verifyBundle, applied atomically before any message in that bundle. No admin key, governance call or ConfigUpdate can touch it. A verifier that returns a new anchor without a state-proven commitment in the same proof MUST revert; that requirement is what prevents anchor injection by a compromised endpoint.

    Rotation evidence is the list of epoch-boundary headers (epochBlockHeaders) in the proof: each is sealed by the previous set and lists the next set in extraData; the verifier walks them in order and returns only the final anchor.     Hiero (TSS) verifier. Rotation is not implemented yet: verifyBundle returns an empty newTrustAnchor, so the anchor is immutable for the Channel's lifetime. One generic Hiero verifier can serve Channels to many Hiero networks; the anchor says which network is being verified.
     
     
     
    What can and cannot change the anchor
     
       
    • The anchor is written by verifyConfig at completeChannel and afterwards changes only when verifyBundle returns a non-empty new_trust_anchor. There is no admin key, governance call or ConfigUpdate path.
      •  
    • Succession requirement: the successor must be committed to inside the same proof that was verified against the current anchor; a verifier that returns a new anchor without that proven commitment must revert. This is what stops a compromised endpoint from injecting an authority.
      •  
    • The Service stores new_trust_anchor and new_trust_anchor_id atomically before processing any message in the bundle (spec §4.2 Step 1c); the id must be non-empty exactly when the anchor is.
      •  
    • A bundle with zero application messages that advances the anchor is valid: Bundle Progress Criterion 2.
      •  
    • ChannelContext (channel_id, service_address) is stored separately and never changes. The ADR rejected putting identity inside the anchor because a field designed to rotate cannot also carry something that must not: it would make a legitimate rotation indistinguishable from an identity substitution.
      •

    The endpoint carries the obligation to keep the remote anchor current. Every sync payload reports the peer’s trust_anchor_id; the endpoint keeps a local ordered record of authorities it has seen and, when it cannot produce proofs the remote would accept, submits transition bundles until it can. Whether timing is tight depends on the anchor’s encoding: a single-authority anchor must be advanced exactly at the boundary, while a window anchor (current, next) decouples submission from the rotation and is recommended for proof systems with frequent, predictable rotations such as Ethereum’s roughly 27-hour sync committee. A proof system that cannot produce a proof verifiable by the old authority and committing to the new one “cannot support the CLPR trust anchor model”.

    Connectors, payment and slashing
     
    Source: design §3.3 · spec §2.2 · spec §3.3 · spec §4.6 · spec §6.3 (clpr-spec@945082d)
     
    The messaging layer moves bytes; the payment layer decides who pays. A connector is a contract registered on a specific channel with a counterpart on the peer ledger under the same derived identity:
    connector_id = keccak256( channel_id ‖ public_key ‖ salt )      // salt defaults to 32 zero bytes

Connector {
  connector_id       : bytes(32)   // primary key with channel_id
  channel_id         : bytes(32)
  connector_contract : bytes       // authorization contract on this ledger
  admin              : bytes       // can top up, adjust, remove
  balance            : uint        // pays for inbound execution
  locked_stake       : uint        // slashable
}
    Registration mirrors channels: commit keccak256(connector_id ‖ public_key), then reveal (public_key, salt, signature over keccak256(connector_id ‖ service_address), connector_contract, initial_balance, stake); the service re-derives the id and checks the commitment. On Hiero, service_address is the system contract 0x…016e. Storage is keyed by (channel_id, connector_id), so a squatter on some third ledger affects only channels there. The relationship is many-to-many, and the requirement is bilateral: a connector registered only on the source side authorizes messages that then fail on the destination with CONNECTOR_NOT_FOUND.
    Authorizing
    interface IClprConnectorAuth {
  function authorizeMessage(bytes sender, bytes target_application,
                            uint64 message_size, bytes message_data) returns (bool authorized)
}
    Returning true is a commitment: “it asserts that its counterpart on the destination ledger has sufficient funds to pay for execution, and that it itself can cover the cost of handling the response. This commitment is the contractual basis for slashing.” A pass-through connector accepts everything; a sophisticated one enforces allow-lists, rate limits or payment from the application. The call MUST NOT modify service state.
    Paying
    On the destination, each Data Message’s connector_id is resolved to the local connector. If it exists and can pay, it is charged actual_gas_used × (1 + margin_percent / 100), and the entire amount goes to the account that submitted the bundle: no treasury, no owner cut, nothing to the sending side. Response Messages are charged the same way on their way home. Then the application is called.

     Not settled yet 
    Stake-to-exposure invariant. “Each side’s Connector bond MUST be sufficient to cover the worst-case endpoint losses on that ledger.” Otherwise a connector with minimal stake can authorize a burst and drain endpoints of more execution cost than the slash reimburses.     The EVM connector surface is IClprConnector with three callbacks: authorizeOutboundMessage(channelId, targetApplication, sender, messageData), payForExecution(amount) (the connector must transfer exactly amount to the caller or be marked underfunded and slashed), and a best-effort onInboundMessage notification under a bounded gas stipend. The implementation charges a configured messageExecutionCost × (1 + margin) rather than actual gas used, enforces a per-connector queue quota (connectorQueueQuotaPct × maxQueueDepth / 100), and applies geometric slashing basePenalty × penaltyMultiplier^slashCount capped at the locked stake, with auto-ban at a threshold or when stake reaches zero. Its authors flag the slashing formula as a candidate for the platform-neutral spec. See docs/evm-platform-spec.md and IClprConnector.sol.
     
     Key point 
    A connector authorizes on the sending ledger and pays on the receiving one; locked stake on both sides backs that promise.

    Security considerations
     
    Source: spec §8 (clpr-spec@945082d)
     
      

    • Strictness (8.1). Published limits make violations attributable; unrecognized data in a bundle is rejected (unknown wire fields, truncated or malformed control data), though a malformed data message gets an error reply.
      • 

    • Trust model (8.2). Verifier, peer consensus, both services. No verifier allow-list exists.
      • 

    • Reorg risk (8.3). Commitment level is the verifier’s property; use finalized for anything irreversible.
      • 

    • Sybil resistance (8.4). Admin curation of the endpoint manifest; the bond is friction, not a threshold; no reputation system for endpoints; the admin is fully trusted not to empty the manifest.
      • 

    • Reentrancy (8.5). Dispatching hands control to arbitrary code. Implementations MUST use reentrancy guards and checks-effects-interactions: all channel state updated before the application is called.
      • 

     In the EVM reference code 
    The EVM contracts rely on a guard on submitBundle instead (ClprService.sol, BundleLib.sol): the application is called first, the connector is charged after, and the channel cursors are written after the whole loop.
     
      

    • Untrusted payloads (8.6) and no confidentiality (8.7).
      • 

    • Upgradeable verifiers (8.8) are valid but shift trust to whoever holds the upgrade key.
      • 

    • Upgradeable services (8.9). Endpoint proof construction depends on storage layout; uncoordinated upgrades halt all syncs. The EVM repository snapshots its storage layout in CI for exactly this reason.
      • 

    • Endpoint pre-funding (8.10). Out-of-funds endpoints are effectively offline.
      • 

    • Queue monopolization (8.11). Open; platform specs SHOULD define quotas, escrow or pricing.
      • 

    Misbehavior detection is strictly local: “there is no cross-ledger misbehavior reporting protocol”.

    CLPR guarantees authenticity and integrity, not semantic correctness and not confidentiality.

    At every layer, the party with economic exposure performs the vetting; the admin, with none, has the weakest incentive to monitor.

    Before you start
    The pattern that makes CLPR extensible
    Nothing on existing ledgers changes when a new one joins. The alignment between the new ledger's endpoint (which builds bundles in that ledger's format) and the verifier it ships for other ledgers (which reads that format) is the whole trick.
     
    Step 1 of 8
    Implement a CLPR Service and CLPR Endpoints for the new ledger type
    The Service can be native to the node or a smart contract; either way it must behave exactly as the specification describes. The Endpoints read the new ledger's CLPR state, build state-proven bundles in the ledger's own data format, and speak the common sync protocol.
    Step 2 of 8
    Implement a Hiero verifier that runs on the new ledger
    So the new ledger can check proofs coming from Hiero.
    Step 3 of 8
    Implement a verifier for the new ledger that runs on Hiero
    So Hiero can check proofs coming from the new ledger. This verifier also translates the new ledger's bundle format into what the receiving Service expects.
    Step 4 of 8
    Deploy each verifier to the opposite network
    Verifiers are ordinary contracts on the receiving side. Deploying one is permissionless.
    Step 5 of 8
    Set up a Channel between the two CLPR Services
    Commit on both ledgers, then reveal on both, naming the verifier each side will use. The verifier is fixed for the channel's lifetime.
    Step 6 of 8
    Share each network's endpoints with the other
    The endpoint list is on-chain state on each side and is proved into the channel at creation; later changes travel inside bundle proofs.
    Step 7 of 8
    Set up a common Connector bonded to the Channel
    The same connector identity is registered on both ledgers: it authorizes on the sending side and pays on the receiving side.
    Step 8 of 8
    Deploy applications that use the Connector and Channel
    Applications implement two callbacks and never touch proofs, relays or verification.

    Endpoints on Hiero
     
    Where: part of the native implementation
     
    On a Hiero network there is no separate endpoint program: every consensus node is automatically an endpoint, and the list of them is kept up to date by the network itself. The node builds and signs the proofs as part of producing blocks; the endpoint only fetches the finished proof and carries it.

    The sync protocol
     
    Where: spec §1.5; clpr-endpoint proto + grpc modules
     
    How two endpoints talk: over mutual TLS, each sync is one stream for one channel, on which each side says how far it has got and receives exactly the messages it is missing, together with the proof. Requests are cheap and unproven; bundles are proven. Nothing is delivered that the receiver did not ask for.
     
    gRPC ClprEndpointService.sync: a bidirectional stream of ClprSyncPayload (channel_id, optional BundleRequest, optional BundleResponse); one stream is one cycle; both fields absent closes it. mTLS with an on-chain ECDSA P-384 CA per endpoint (≤ 512 bytes) and a per-process Ed25519 leaf. Message sizes bounded by max_sync_bytes. Defined in clpr-relay-proto and served by clpr-relay-grpc-server.

    How the specification evolves
     
    Source: ADR guide · options to consider (clpr-spec@945082d)
     
    The text comes in two parts. The design document (clpr-service.md) explains the reasoning: the problem, the layers, the participants, the trust model, and what happens in each failure. The protocol specification (clpr-service-spec.md) is the normative part every implementation is measured against: the exact bytes on the wire, the on-ledger state, the step-by-step algorithms, and a language-independent description of every operation a service offers. Next to them sit one folder per proof system under verifiers/, the decision records under ADR/, and the list of options under consideration.
    Three rules govern change. Any change to the bytes that travel between ledgers raises the protocol version, and two ledgers must agree on it or stop talking. Data an implementation does not recognize is rejected, never skipped. And the reference implementations keep their own written list of where they still differ from the text.
    Changes to the specification are made through Architecture Decision Records, named YYYY-MM-DD-short-title.md, with a fixed final form: an abstract ending in an enumerated “Changes in this ADR”, a problem description per discrete problem, the options considered and non-goals, the complete solution, and the exact edits to each specification file.
    Only final-form ADRs are merged to main. Five exist:
     
     
    ADRDecision
      
    2026-07-02Add an immutable ChannelContext (channel id, peer service address) to the verifier interface, separate from the rotating trust anchor, so EVM/QBFT verifiers can derive storage slots and provenance cannot be misattributed.
    2026-07-03Move endpoint lists out of ConfigUpdate into a proven, versioned on-ledger manifest refreshed through bundle payloads, fixing the complete-turnover deadlock; two-step admission with bond on permissionless ledgers.
    2026-07-14Replace the unary sync RPC with a bidirectional-streaming request/response cycle that eliminates systematic duplicate sends; adopt a P-384 root CA / Ed25519 leaf mTLS model.
    2026-07-17Rename ClprQueueMetadata to a minimal, separately provable ChannelSyncData; allow bundles to carry a proper prefix of the backlog.
    2026-08-01Remove message redaction: redacted and unredacted encodings were hash-equivalent, so any permissionless submitter could flip them undetected. Simplifies the running hash.

If you are looking for somewhere to contribute, that list is the map.
     Key point 
    Only a final-form ADR, one that enumerates the exact edits to each specification file, is merged to main.

    Application
     
    A contract implementing IClprApplication: onClprMessage and onClprResponse. It sends by calling IClprService.sendMessage.
     
    in plain words
    The program that wants to talk to another ledger. It implements two callbacks: one for when a message arrives, one for when a reply comes back.
     
     
    CLPR Service
     
    The core on-ledger component: single source of truth for all CLPR state, holder of channels, connectors, endpoint manifests and locked funds. Native to the node on Hiero; a smart contract on EVM ledgers.
     
    in plain words
    The protocol's own part of each ledger. It keeps the outbound queues, checks the proofs, routes messages to applications, and handles the money.
     
     
    Channel
     
    An on-ledger entity representing a communication path to one specific peer CLPR Service, bound to one verifier contract for its lifetime. Many channels may exist between the same two ledgers.
     
    in plain words
    A standing agreement between two ledgers to exchange messages, with its own numbered queue in each direction and a fixed way of checking the other side's proofs.
     
     
    Verifier contract
     
    An immutable contract implementing verifyConfig and verifyBundle. It authenticates state proofs from the peer against the channel's trust anchor and decodes bundle content into the local format.
     
    in plain words
    The specialist that knows how to check one particular ledger's signatures and proofs. Each channel picks one when it is created and can never change it.
     
     
    Trust anchor
     
    Opaque, verifier-defined bytes encoding the peer ledger's current signing authority. For Hiero: the ledger_id. For QBFT: the validator set hash plus epoch data. Rotated only through verifyBundle.
     
    in plain words
    Who is allowed to sign for the other ledger right now. Stored per channel and only ever updated by a proof that the previous signers approved the change.
     
     
    Connector
     
    An economic entity registered on both ledgers under the same derived id. Authorizes outbound messages, funds inbound execution, and is subject to slashing for connector-attributable failures.
     
    in plain words
    The account that pays. It approves a message on the sending side and pays for running it on the receiving side. If it breaks that promise, it is fined.
     
     
    Endpoint
     
    A node running the sync protocol: exchanging bundles with peer endpoints over gRPC, obtaining state proofs, pre-verifying, and submitting bundles. On Hiero every consensus node is one; elsewhere endpoints register and post a bond.
     
    in plain words
    The program that carries messages between ledgers. It watches its own ledger, collects proofs, talks to endpoints on the other side, and submits what it receives as an ordinary transaction. It cannot forge anything.
     
     
    Message
     
    ClprMessagePayload: a oneof of ClprMessage (Data), ClprMessageReply (Response) or ClprControlMessage (Control). Every Data Message produces exactly one Response Message, in order.
     
    in plain words
    A parcel of bytes plus an address. Three kinds share one queue: data (what applications send), responses (the answer to a data message), and control (protocol housekeeping).
     
     
    Bundle
     
    An ordered batch of message payloads plus a state proof over the anchor message. The running hash chain extends the proof to every message in the batch.
     
    in plain words
    A batch of messages that travels together with one proof covering the last of them.
     
     
    State proof
     
    A cryptographic proof that specific data exists in a ledger's committed state, verified against the trust anchor. State proofs are CLPR's sole mechanism of cross-ledger trust.
     
    in plain words
    A sealed receipt from a whole network, showing that a specific piece of data really is in its records.
     
     
    Running hash
     
    SHA-256(previous_running_hash ‖ serialized_payload), initialised to 32 zero bytes, kept separately for sent and received messages.
     
    in plain words
    A hash of everything ever sent on a channel, updated with each message so that nothing can be inserted, removed or reordered unnoticed.
     
     
    CLPR Service admin
     
    Exclusively protective authority: configure and close. Cannot create channels, register connectors or take part in economic activity. The network operator or governing body on Hiero; whoever controls the CLPR Service contract on EVM.
     
    in plain words
    The emergency brake. Can change the ledger's published limits, admit and evict endpoints, and close channels.
     
     
    Plus a few terms that only appear on this page: anchor message, the last message in a bundle, whose running hash the state proof covers; acknowledgement, the peer’s received_message_id as reported in ChannelSyncData, distinct from a response; close-notification, the final bundle an endpoint owes the remote once its side reaches CLOSED; transition bundle, a bundle whose proof advances the trust anchor, possibly carrying no messages; NoProgress, the rejection of a bundle satisfying none of the five progress criteria; HashSphere, a private or permissioned network running Hiero; CAIP-2, the namespace:reference chain identifier standard used for chain_id.
