Act I · The problem

Why two ledgers cannot just talk

Know what a ledger is, why two of them cannot see each other, and why the usual fix makes every message only as safe as the party in the middle.

Section 1of 16

A ledger is a shared record

Before cross-ledger anything, one idea has to be solid: what a ledger is. Not the finance kind, the blockchain kind, and the two words are close enough to cause trouble.

A ledger, here, is a shared record that many computers keep identical copies of. Nobody owns it. When someone wants to add a line, every computer gets the proposal, they compare notes, and the line is only written once enough of them agree. The rules for “enough” differ from network to network, but the result is always the same: one more line that every copy has and that nobody can quietly change afterwards.

New term

Consensus is the process of the computers agreeing on the next line. The computers doing the agreeing are called validators (on some networks, consensus nodes). Finality is the moment after which a line cannot be undone.

Two consequences matter for everything that follows. First, a sealed line is a fact about the whole network, not about one computer. Second, a network can vouch for a line: its validators can put a joint signature on a short summary of the whole ledger, and anyone who knows the network’s public signing identity can check that signature. A state proof is that signed summary plus the path from the summary down to one particular line. Hold that thought.

Key point

A sealed line is the whole network’s word, not one computer’s, and the network can sign for it.

Check yourself: Why can't a single computer change a line that has already been sealed?

Because every other copy still has the original, and the network only trusts what enough copies agree on. One dissenting copy is simply overruled, as the simulation shows when you tick the box.

Section 2of 16

Ledgers cannot see each other

Now take two of these networks. Each is a world of its own. Nothing written in one ledger is visible from inside the other. A program running on Ledger A cannot look up a balance on Ledger B, cannot call a program there, cannot even tell whether Ledger B exists.

That blindness is by design. A ledger only writes a line if every validator can check it on its own. “Go and look at the other network” fails that test: hundreds of machines cannot all look and be sure they saw the same thing at the same moment. So the two islands stay islands.

But people keep wanting to build things that span them: lock a token on one ledger and issue a matching one on another, let a program on a private network ask a public one to do something, settle a trade whose two legs live on different chains. Every one of those needs a message to cross the water in a way the receiving ledger can rely on.

Gotcha

“But both networks are on the internet.” True, and irrelevant. Being reachable is not the same as being verifiable. The receiving ledger does not need a way to hear about Ledger A; it needs a way to know that what it heard is true. That is the whole subject of this page.

Section 3of 16

The usual fixes, and why they keep failing

The obvious solution is to appoint someone to do the looking. Call them a relayer, an oracle, a committee, a bridge. They watch Ledger A, and when something relevant happens they tell Ledger B, signing the report with a key that Ledger B has been told to trust.

That works, in the sense that messages arrive. The problem is what Ledger B is actually trusting. It is not trusting Ledger A. It is trusting whoever holds that key. If the key is stolen, or the committee members collude, or the program that produces the signature has a bug, Ledger B will accept a message that Ledger A never sent, and it has no way to notice.

Key point

Ledger B never sees Ledger A. It sees a signature from whoever holds the key.

Pick a design, then try an attack. The question is always the same: what can a dishonest middleman make Ledger B believe?

Adding more keys raises the price of the attack. It does not change its shape: there is still a party in the middle whose failure Ledger B cannot detect, and every message is exactly as safe as that party. CLPR sets out to remove the party altogether.

In plain English

A bridge makes every message as safe as the bridge. The goal is to make every message as safe as the two ledgers themselves, and not one bit less.

Check yourself: In the committee design, what exactly does Ledger B check when a message arrives?

Only that three of the five committee keys signed it. It never sees anything from Ledger A. Whether the committee told the truth is not something Ledger B can find out.

Act II · The idea

Proof instead of trust

Replace the party in the middle with a proof the receiving ledger checks itself, see what that leaves out, and meet everyone a message passes through.

Section 4of 16

What CLPR does instead: proof, not trust

CLPR (“Clipper”, for Cross Ledger PRotocol) starts from the trick in section 1. Ledger A’s validators already sign summaries of their ledger as part of sealing lines. So instead of asking a middleman “what happened on A?”, Ledger B is handed a state proof: the validators’ signature on a summary, plus the path from that summary down to the exact line that says “Ann’s program sent this message”. Ledger B checks that the signature really is Ledger A’s, using the signing identity it keeps on file for A. Then it follows the path down to the line. Only when both hold does it believe the message.

The party carrying the proof is still there; CLPR calls it an endpoint: a program that moves messages and their proofs between ledgers. But look at what it can and cannot do. It can deliver, delay, or refuse. It cannot forge a message, because none of its own keys mean anything to Ledger B. It cannot change a message either, because the changed message would no longer match the proof that came with it. Even with dishonest endpoints, no fabricated message can be accepted; whether messages keep arriving at all depends on at least one honest endpoint on each side.

New term

A state proof is a cryptographic proof that a specific piece of data really is in a ledger’s sealed record. In CLPR, state proofs carry the evidence of what is in another ledger’s record; trust also rests on the verifier chosen for the channel.

A verifier is the specialist on the receiving ledger that knows how to check one particular kind of proof. A Hiero network and a Besu network running QBFT (a different way for validators to agree) sign things in completely different ways, so each needs its own verifier. Every channel picks one verifier when it is created and can never swap it.

The trust anchor is what the verifier checks against: the current signing identity of the other ledger, stored per channel. It changes in one way only: the current signers sign a proof that names their replacements.

In plain English

Compare a bank that keeps your signature card on file. A cheque is accepted because the signature on it matches the card, not because the person who brought it in seems trustworthy. The endpoint is the person who brought it in. The trust anchor is the bank’s card.

Removing the middleman does not remove trust. It shrinks it to a short list you can read. An application using a channel trusts its own ledger, the CLPR software on both ledgers, the other ledger’s validators, the application on the other side, and the verifier the channel was created with. That last one deserves attention, because anyone can create a channel with any verifier they like. A channel existing does not mean its verifier is honest. Choosing a channel means choosing to trust its verifier, and applications, and whoever pays for their messages, are expected to check it before using the channel.

Key point

The endpoint can delay a message. It cannot invent one, and it cannot change one.

Check yourself: An endpoint's server is hacked and its private keys are stolen. What can the attacker now make Ledger B believe?

Nothing new. The endpoint’s keys are only used to talk to other endpoints. What Ledger B verifies is Ledger A’s validators’ signature, which the attacker does not have. The worst the attacker can do is stop or slow deliveries from that endpoint, and any other endpoint can take over.

Section 5of 16

What makes CLPR different

One idea, proof instead of trust, has consequences all over the design. Set beside the usual approaches, they come out as six things CLPR does without.

The first two are the point of the previous sections: nothing sits between the ledgers, and the receiving ledger does its own checking. The next two follow from them. There is no new token, because every fee, margin and fine is paid in a ledger’s configured fee asset; and there is no pool of deposited assets, because what travels is a message, not money held somewhere in the middle. The fifth is what makes CLPR a pattern rather than a pair of chains: any ledger can join once a verifier exists for its proofs, and the protocol itself does not change. The sixth is the one to remember when something goes wrong: an endpoint that is hacked, bribed or switched off can delay messages, and that is all it can do.

One more property is easy to miss. If both ledgers are of the kind that keeps working correctly even when some of their own participants misbehave, the messages between them inherit that property, because nothing weaker has been added in between.

Key point

The receiving ledger checks the sending ledger’s own signature. Everything CLPR does without follows from that one decision.

Check yourself: A connector, the account that pays for a message, is also an intermediary. Why is it not 'a party in the middle' in the sense that matters?

Because it cannot make either ledger believe anything. A connector approves and pays; it does not carry or verify a message. If it refuses to pay, the message fails with a reply saying so, and nothing false has been accepted anywhere.

Section 6of 16

The cast

Before watching a message travel, meet everyone it will pass through. Each card gives the everyday description first; open it for the precise one.

Application

The program that wants to talk to another ledger. It implements two callbacks: one for when a message arrives, one for when a reply comes back.

as the spec puts it
A contract implementing IClprApplication: onClprMessage and onClprResponse. It sends by calling IClprService.sendMessage.

CLPR Service

The protocol's own part of each ledger. It keeps the outbound queues, checks the proofs, routes messages to applications, and handles the money.

as the spec puts it
The core on-ledger component: single source of truth for all CLPR state, holder of channels, connectors, endpoint manifests and locked funds. Native to the node on Hiero; a smart contract on EVM ledgers.

Channel

A standing agreement between two ledgers to exchange messages, with its own numbered queue in each direction and a fixed way of checking the other side's proofs.

as the spec puts it
An on-ledger entity representing a communication path to one specific peer CLPR Service, bound to one verifier contract for its lifetime. Many channels may exist between the same two ledgers.

Verifier contract

The specialist that knows how to check one particular ledger's signatures and proofs. Each channel picks one when it is created and can never change it.

as the spec puts it
An immutable contract implementing verifyConfig and verifyBundle. It authenticates state proofs from the peer against the channel's trust anchor and decodes bundle content into the local format.

Trust anchor

Who is allowed to sign for the other ledger right now. Stored per channel and only ever updated by a proof that the previous signers approved the change.

as the spec puts it
Opaque, verifier-defined bytes encoding the peer ledger's current signing authority. For Hiero: the ledger_id. For QBFT: the validator set hash plus epoch data. Rotated only through verifyBundle.

Connector

The account that pays. It approves a message on the sending side and pays for running it on the receiving side. If it breaks that promise, it is fined.

as the spec puts it
An economic entity registered on both ledgers under the same derived id. Authorizes outbound messages, funds inbound execution, and is subject to slashing for connector-attributable failures.

Endpoint

The program that carries messages between ledgers. It watches its own ledger, collects proofs, talks to endpoints on the other side, and submits what it receives as an ordinary transaction. It cannot forge anything.

as the spec puts it
A node running the sync protocol: exchanging bundles with peer endpoints over gRPC, obtaining state proofs, pre-verifying, and submitting bundles. On Hiero every consensus node is one; elsewhere endpoints register and post a bond.

Message

A parcel of bytes plus an address. Three kinds share one queue: data (what applications send), responses (the answer to a data message), and control (protocol housekeeping).

as the spec puts it
ClprMessagePayload: a oneof of ClprMessage (Data), ClprMessageReply (Response) or ClprControlMessage (Control). Every Data Message produces exactly one Response Message, in order.

Bundle

A batch of messages that travels together with one proof covering the last of them.

as the spec puts it
An ordered batch of message payloads plus a state proof over the anchor message. The running hash chain extends the proof to every message in the batch.

State proof

A sealed receipt from a whole network, showing that a specific piece of data really is in its records.

as the spec puts it
A cryptographic proof that specific data exists in a ledger's committed state, verified against the trust anchor. State proofs are CLPR's sole mechanism of cross-ledger trust.

Running hash

A hash of everything ever sent on a channel, updated with each message so that nothing can be inserted, removed or reordered unnoticed.

as the spec puts it
SHA-256(previous_running_hash ‖ serialized_payload), initialised to 32 zero bytes, kept separately for sent and received messages.

CLPR Service admin

The emergency brake. Can change the ledger's published limits, admit and evict endpoints, and close channels.

as the spec puts it
Exclusively protective authority: configure and close. Cannot create channels, register connectors or take part in economic activity. The network operator or governing body on Hiero; whoever controls the CLPR Service contract on EVM.
Hiero and Hedera

Two names come up constantly. Hiero is the open-source ledger software: the node, its APIs, its state model. Hedera is the public network that runs it; private networks running the same software are called HashSpheres. On Hiero, the CLPR Service is part of the node software itself. On EVM-compatible ledgers, such as Besu networks, it is a smart contract instead: a program deployed on the ledger like any other. Same protocol, same rules, two implementations, and the simulations below show one of each.

The message journey, step by step

Here is the whole thing at once, and it is the heart of this page. On the left, a ledger running the Hiero software. On the right, an EVM ledger whose validators seal blocks by a method called QBFT consensus. An application on the left sends the word “ping” to its twin on the right; the twin echoes it back. Twenty-eight steps, two proofs, one reply. The switch at the top runs the same trip in the other direction, which proves itself with different cryptography.

The connector is the account that pays. The service asks it, in effect: are you willing to cover this one? If it says no, nothing is sent.

If anything about this ledger's published limits changed since the last time this channel told the other side, a small housekeeping message is slipped into the queue ahead of yours. On a normal day nothing has changed and this step does nothing.

The message is written into the outbound queue and given the next number in line. It is also folded into a running hash of everything ever sent on this channel, so the other side can later prove that nothing was inserted, removed or reordered.

The endpoint collects the receipt proving the message really is in the ledger's signed state. It only fetches this proof. It does not create it, and it could not forge it if it wanted to.

Before spending money submitting anything, the receiving endpoint runs the whole verification locally as a dry run. If it would fail, nobody pays for it.

The endpoint sends an ordinary transaction on the destination ledger carrying the message and its proof. Everything from here until the reply is queued happens inside this single transaction.

The verifier checks the threshold signature and the Merkle paths

The destination ledger checks the source ledger's signature itself, against the identity it already trusts, and walks the proof back to the signed block. This is the step that means nobody is trusting a bridge operator.

Then the bookkeeping. Is this the next message in sequence? Have we seen it before? Does recomputing the running hash give the same answer as the proven one? Any mismatch and it stops here.

Crossing · step 15 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

The destination looks up the connector that agreed to pay and works out how much execution it can afford.

Crossing · step 16 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

The application receives the message and echoes it

The application on the other ledger finally receives the message. It announces it and hands the same payload straight back as its answer. This is the moment the message has actually crossed; everything before it was transport.

Crossing · step 17 of 28 · Hiero → EVM ledger

The connector is charged and the submitter reimbursed

The connector pays for the execution that just happened, plus a little extra, and the whole amount goes to whoever submitted the transaction. That margin is why anyone bothers running an endpoint.

The answer is placed in the destination ledger's own outbound queue, numbered and folded into the running hash the same way the original was. Every message that gets accepted produces exactly one reply.

Three things stand out after watching it. None of them, usefully. Steps 7 to 11 and 20 to 21 are the endpoint’s own work, but everything it produces is checked at steps 13 to 14 and 23 to 24 against signatures it cannot make. A tampered bundle is rejected and the endpoint has paid a transaction fee for nothing.

Section 8of 16

One proof for a whole batch

Step 6 of the journey mentioned a running hash. It is simple and it does a lot of work.

Every message added to a channel’s queue is given the next number in line and a running hash: a hash computed from the previous running hash and the message itself. (A hash is a short digest of some data; change one character of the data and the digest comes out completely different.) Change any message, drop one, or swap two, and every running hash after that point comes out different. The receiving ledger continues the chain from its own last running hash over the new messages it was handed and compares the final value with the one Ledger A’s validators signed. The numbers below are real SHA-256 hashes, computed in your browser.

This is why an endpoint can carry a batch of, say, forty messages with a proof covering only the last one. The receiver continues the chain from its last stored hash over the new messages it was handed, and if its last running hash matches the proven one, every message in the batch is proven too. The numbering does a second job: the receiver remembers the last number it processed and skips messages it already has, rejecting a bundle that jumps ahead or carries nothing new, so an old bundle cannot be delivered twice even though its proof is still perfectly valid.

Key point

Prove the last running hash and recompute the rest: one proof covers the whole batch, and one mismatch rejects it.

Section 9of 16

Who pays, and who gets paid

Somebody has to pay for all this. Delivering a message means an endpoint submits a transaction on the receiving ledger, and that transaction runs the application there. Submitting that transaction costs a fee, which ledgers call gas; endpoints are not charities.

CLPR introduces no new token. Instead it has a role called the connector: an account that agrees to pay. The same connector is registered on both ledgers under one identity. On the sending side the connector is asked, before the message is even queued, whether it will cover this one. On the receiving side the connector is charged for the gas the application actually used, plus a margin, and the whole amount goes to whoever submitted the bundle. That margin is the entire reason anyone runs an endpoint.

The connector’s approval on the sending side is a promise: my counterpart on the other ledger can pay for this, and I can pay for the reply. A connector locks up a deposit in advance, its stake. If the promise turns out to be false, the reply that comes home says so, and the fine is taken from that stake. Fines grow with each failure; a connector that keeps failing is banned from the channel and loses whatever stake is left.

Key point

No new token and no treasury: the connector pays the endpoint that delivered, and a connector that promised but could not pay is fined from its stake.

Gotcha

An application that crashes on the receiving side is not the connector’s fault and is not fined. The connector promised to pay for execution, and it did; the application simply reverted. The reply carries an “application error” and the sending application decides what to do. Only two outcomes are the connector’s fault: it does not exist on the other side, or it cannot pay.

Applications choose their connector, and connectors can be as picky as they like: allow-lists, rate limits, charging a fee to the application. Several connectors can serve the same channel, and one connector can serve many channels. Connectors, more than anyone, are expected to check a channel’s verifier before using it: if the verifier is bad, the money at risk is the connector’s locked stake.

Not settled yet

Two things about the money are not settled yet: how large a connector’s stake must be so that a fine always covers what an endpoint lost, and how to stop one connector filling a shared queue and starving the others. Per-connector quotas, escrow at send time and priority pricing are the candidate answers. The EVM reference code enforces a per-connector quota today.

Check yourself: Who is paid when a message is delivered, and who is not?

Only the account that submitted the bundle on the receiving ledger, and only from the connector. Nobody on the sending side is paid for anything, there is no protocol treasury, and the endpoint that did the sending work earns nothing directly. Endpoints are expected to prefer partners that bring them messages in return.

Notice a pattern. Almost every failure is handled by rejecting a bundle and changing nothing: the channel stays open and the next honest bundle goes through. One failure is treated as a sign that something is broken rather than a passing glitch: replies arriving out of order. For that one, the channel is paused instead of guessing. In plain English

Two words used precisely throughout: safety (nothing false is ever accepted) and liveness (things keep moving). In CLPR, safety rests on the two ledgers and their verifiers, so there is no middleman who could be bribed or hacked into accepting something false. Liveness is different: it depends on somebody, anybody, running an endpoint.

Key point

Almost every failure is answered by rejecting a bundle and changing nothing; the only failures that cost money are a connector’s broken promises.

Four things people build with it

Everything so far is plumbing. Here is what people build on it. A message in CLPR is just bytes with an address, and every message gets exactly one reply, so the same primitive serves four quite different jobs.

Remote smart contract call

A contract on one ledger calls a proxy, which records the call. An endpoint carries it across, the proxy on the other ledger invokes the target contract, and the result comes back the same way. Applications have to be written for answers that arrive later, not in the same call.

Escrow, mint, then burn

Assets are locked on the source ledger, then minted on the destination. If minting succeeds the locked assets may be burned; if it fails they are released. Needs atomic semantics and a common asset standard.

N-ledger atomic swap

Several ledgers make state changes tied together by two-phase commit: each enters a ready-or-roll-back state, and if any one fails, all roll back. Every participating ledger must understand the others' commit messages.

Native Hiero asset management

Hiero tokens, fungible and non-fungible, handled across Hiero networks by the messaging layer directly, with no smart-contract proxies in between.

When several channels exist between the same two ledgers, for example one per Ethereum commitment level, the application picks one at send time by its own needs: a price feed may accept the risk of a reorganisation for speed, a high-value transfer waits for finality. CLPR imposes no default.

The third pattern is worth watching, because it shows what the protocol does and does not do. Three ledgers are meant to change together or not at all. CLPR carries the messages and guarantees each one a reply; the decision to commit or roll back lives in the application that started the swap. The simulation draws one way such an application could use the protocol; the choreography is the site’s illustration, the pattern is the protocol’s.

Because CLPR knows nothing about swaps. It knows messages, replies, proofs and payment. Tying three ledgers’ changes together is a meaning the application gives to its messages, and only the application can decide what “everyone is ready” means for it.

Shapes of a network

A channel always joins exactly two ledgers. That sounds limiting. It is the opposite: because every pair agrees on its own, with its own verifiers and its own queues, any shape you can draw from pairs is a shape you can build, and no shape needs anyone in the middle.

The one thing to keep straight is that CLPR itself knows only the pairs. Nothing in the protocol routes a message from one ledger to a third through a second. In the star, a message from one spoke to another has no path, unless an application on the hub receives it and sends a new message of its own to the other spoke, under its own connector and its own channel. That is a perfectly good thing to build; it is an application, not a protocol feature.

Adding a new kind of ledger

CLPR is a pattern, not a pair of chains. Adding a new kind of ledger needs no change to the protocol or to existing implementations; each ledger it connects to deploys the new verifier and registers a channel. It needs three things. First, a CLPR Service on the new ledger. Second, endpoints that can build proofs in the new ledger’s format. Third, a pair of verifiers: one runs on the new ledger and checks the existing ledger’s proofs; the other runs on the existing ledger and checks the new ledger’s proofs.

Section 15of 16

What CLPR does not do

A page like this should be as clear about limits as about mechanisms.

Delivery depends on endpoints. If nobody runs an endpoint for a channel, messages sit in the queue. Nothing is lost or faked; nothing moves either.

Nothing is secret. Message contents sit on both ledgers in plain view. The protocol guarantees that a message is authentic and unaltered, not that it is private. Applications that need confidentiality must encrypt before sending; even then, when a message was sent, how big it is and who sent it stay visible.

Authentic is not the same as safe. CLPR proves a message really came from the other ledger. It says nothing about whether the message is sensible. An application must treat everything that arrives from across the water as untrusted input, exactly as it would treat a call from a stranger.

Key point

CLPR proves a message is real; it does not prove it is private, sensible or safe to act on.

A channel is only as good as its verifier. Anyone can create a channel with any verifier. The protocol’s own checks catch a buggy verifier that returns garbled messages, because the running hashes will not match; they cannot catch a lying verifier that fabricates messages and matching running hashes. Vetting verifiers is human work.

Some ledgers can rewrite recent history. On some networks a block is not final the moment it is made; for a while the network can still drop it and replace it. A verifier that accepts a block that fresh risks a “phantom message”: one delivered on this side whose sending block the source ledger later threw away. Each channel chooses how final a block must be, and the safe setting is recommended for anything valuable; two channels between the same ledgers can choose differently.

Some things are not settled yet. These are the open questions:

  • Anyone can register a channel claiming any chain id. Channel creation needs nobody's permission, so anyone can register a ledger configuration with any chain id they like. An application has to check for itself that a channel really leads to the ledger it intends; a channel's existence proves nothing about its peer.
  • Open endpoint registration rests on admin curation. Where anyone can register an endpoint, keeping the list honest rests mainly on the admin admitting each one; the bond adds friction but is not sized against attack costs on any particular chain. Peers should be picked with some randomness so no two endpoints pair up for good. On Hiero the network's own stake-weighted consensus covers this.
  • A verifier behind an upgradeable proxy can change under a channel. The verifier's address is fixed for a channel's life, but the contract at that address may be a proxy whose logic can be replaced without the protocol noticing. The protocol allows it; anyone evaluating a channel should check whether its verifier is a proxy and who holds the upgrade key.
  • How large a connector's stake must be is not yet quantified. A connector's locked stake must cover the worst case: on the receiving side, the cost of running in-flight messages from a connector that has run dry; on the sending side, the fines for failed deliveries. A bond that is too small lets an attacker drain endpoints faster than fines can repay them. The minimum is a design parameter still to be quantified before production.
  • One connector could fill a shared queue. A single connector could approve enough messages to fill a channel's queue and block everyone else on it. Candidate answers: money escrowed per message and returned when the reply arrives, a per-connector share of the queue, or prices that rise as the queue fills. Unresolved before production.
  • The admin has duties but no reward. The service admin must close a compromised channel promptly and gains nothing in the protocol for doing so: a known gap between responsibility and motivation.

Beyond these, the authors keep a separate list of possible changes they have considered and not decided.

The protocol is young. The initial text is expected to change further to polish out rough edges, with every change recorded as a dated decision record saying what was decided and why.

Where CLPR lives, and where this leaves you

CLPR is a set of ideas and also a set of files. Everything the lab publishes is one of four kinds of thing. The specification says what must happen. The CLPR Service, the on-ledger part that holds every channel and queue, is built twice: once into Hiero nodes, once as contracts for EVM networks. The verifiers plug into a service. And the endpoint is the program that runs off the ledgers and carries bundles between them.

The specification

Where: LFDT-CLPR/clpr-spec

The rulebook every other piece must follow. Two documents: a design document that explains the why (trust model, layers, roles, recovery) and a protocol specification that fixes the what (wire formats, state, algorithms, a pseudo-API). Alongside them, one folder per supported proof system saying exactly how that ledger's proofs are built and checked, and a record of every decision that changed the text.

Pinned: design document · protocol specification · verifiers/ · ADR/

CLPR Service, native (Hiero)

Where: hiero-ledger/hiero-consensus-node, feature branch

On Hiero networks the CLPR Service is built into the node software itself. Its queues and channels live in the node's own state tree, which is what makes them provable with the network's own signature. Contracts reach it through a fixed system address.

Pinned: org profile: initial OSS · design §3.1.0

CLPR Service, contract (EVM)

Where: LFDT-CLPR/clpr-smart-contracts

On EVM-compatible ledgers such as Besu networks the CLPR Service is a set of Solidity contracts. One immutable front door holds all the state and forwards each call to a module that knows how to handle it: channels, messaging, bundles, connectors, administration. Built and tested with Foundry.

Pinned: ClprService.sol · docs/architecture.md · IClprService.sol · README

Verifier contracts

Where: LFDT-CLPR/clpr-smart-contracts/src/verifiers

The specialists that check another ledger's proofs. Each channel names one when it is created and can never change it. Two are documented as production in the spec: one for Hiero networks (checking the network's joint threshold signature) and one for Besu QBFT networks (checking validator seals on block headers plus storage proofs). The repository also carries verifier code for other consensus families.

Pinned: HieroVerifier.sol · QBFTVerifier.sol · IClprVerifier.sol · spec verifiers/

Endpoint (relay) for EVM ledgers

Where: LFDT-CLPR/clpr-endpoint

The endpoint program for EVM ledgers. It watches its own ledger's CLPR Service, talks to endpoints on the other side over gRPC, builds proofs of what its ledger has queued, dry-runs every bundle it receives, and submits the good ones as ordinary transactions. It never holds a key that any ledger trusts for anything but paying its own transaction fees.

Pinned: README · docs/architecture.md · docs/sync-protocol.md · relay-config-example.yaml

Endpoints on Hiero

Where: part of the native implementation

On a Hiero network there is no separate endpoint program: every consensus node is automatically an endpoint, and the list of them is kept up to date by the network itself. The node builds and signs the proofs as part of producing blocks; the endpoint only fetches the finished proof and carries it.

Pinned: design §3.1.2 · verifiers/hiero/state_proof.md

The sync protocol

Where: spec §1.5; clpr-endpoint proto + grpc modules

How two endpoints talk: over mutual TLS, each sync is one stream for one channel, on which each side says how far it has got and receives exactly the messages it is missing, together with the proof. Requests are cheap and unproven; bundles are proven. Nothing is delivered that the receiver did not ask for.

Pinned: spec §1.5 · ADR 2026-07-14 · clpr-relay-proto

Connector contracts

Where: IClprConnector (EVM); native registration on Hiero

The account that pays, present on both ledgers under one identity. On the sending side its contract decides whether to approve a message; on the receiving side its balance pays for running the message and its locked stake is what gets fined if it breaks its promise. Anyone can register one, on any channel, with a balance and a stake.

Pinned: IClprConnector.sol · design §3.3.1 · spec §6.3

Applications

Where: IClprApplication; anything you deploy

Your program. It calls one function to send and implements two callbacks to receive: one for an incoming message (whatever it returns becomes the reply) and one for the reply to a message it sent. Everything else, proofs, endpoints, verification and payment, is handled below it.

Pinned: IClprApplication.sol · E2EApplication.sol · design §3.4

The lab: LF Decentralized Trust

Where: LFDT-CLPR/governance and .github

Where all of this is governed: an incubation space under the Linux Foundation's LF Decentralized Trust, donated by the Hedera Governing Council with Hashgraph as initial steward, open to anyone under the Apache License 2.0 with a Developer Certificate of Origin sign-off on every contribution.

The two CLPR Services have no code in common. They agree because both follow the same text, down to the exact bytes a message is written as and the exact order of the checks; where code and text disagree, the text wins and the code is fixed, and the contracts repository keeps a written list of where it currently falls short.

The ideas come from Dr. Leemon Baird, co-founder of Hedera and designer of the hashgraph consensus algorithm, and from a practical need: Hiero-based networks, public ones such as Hedera and private ones called HashSpheres, needed a fast way to send each other messages that stay correct even when some participants misbehave and that never make one network wait on the other. Under its agreement with the Hedera Governing Council, Hashgraph wrote the initial text and built the three pieces of software above; the Council wants to donate CLPR if the Lab proposal is accepted, including the brand, to be incubated as an LF Decentralized Trust lab, a lightweight workspace under the Linux Foundation where stewards check that work is novel, on-mission and properly licensed, and explicitly do not review its code. The CLPR Service built into Hiero nodes is not yet part of the main Hiero code: it sits on a separate branch, approved by the Hedera Governing Council and still pending the Hiero Technical Steering Committee.

If you remember six things, remember these.

  1. A ledger is a shared record many machines keep in sync, and a network can jointly sign a proof that a particular line is in it.
  2. Two ledgers cannot see each other, and the usual fix, a trusted party in the middle, makes every message only as safe as that party.
  3. CLPR replaces the party with a proof: the receiving ledger checks the sending ledger’s own validators’ signature, using a verifier fixed to the channel.
  4. Endpoints carry proofs but hold no trusted keys. They can delay; they cannot forge. Whether a message is genuine is decided on the ledgers (safety); whether it arrives at all depends on endpoints (liveness).
  5. Messages are numbered and chained into a running hash, so one proof covers a whole batch and nothing can be inserted, dropped, reordered or replayed.
  6. Connectors pay, endpoints are reimbursed with a margin, and a connector that breaks its promise is fined. Every accepted message gets exactly one reply.
Key point

Proof, not trust: the receiving ledger believes a message because it checked the sending ledger’s own validators’ signature, not because it trusts whoever carried it.
